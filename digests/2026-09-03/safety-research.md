# Research Papers (2026-09-03)

## Key Papers

**[The Implications of Linguistic Illegibility for LLM Security](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02852v1)** introduces the concept of "linguistic illegibility" - scenarios where LLM outputs and mechanistically-probed features fail to represent internal computation. This challenges fundamental assumptions in AI safety evaluation and interpretability research. The work highlights a critical gap between what we can observe about LLM reasoning and what actually drives model behavior, with significant implications for safety assessments.

**[SafeEvolve: Harness-Policy Co-Evolution from Agent Experience for Safety Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02786v1)** addresses the challenge that LLM-based agents face safety risks in both individual responses and multi-step execution trajectories. The proposed framework enables experience-driven self-evolution of both safety policies and runtime harnesses, moving beyond isolated safety mechanisms toward integrated alignment across agent execution. This represents a significant advance in making autonomous agents safer in production environments.

**[A Finger on the Scale: Covert Policy Steering through Agentic Skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02564v1)** formalizes a new supply-chain security risk where third-party skills can preserve declared functionality while covertly redirecting agent decisions. The authors introduce "Skill Policy Integrity" as a requirement and demonstrate how skills can act as externalized behavioral policies that create hidden attack surfaces. This work reveals a fundamental vulnerability in modular agent architectures that rely on external skill components.

**[SEAL: Reinforcing Global Safety in Mixture-of-Experts through Shared Expert ALignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02293v1)** identifies a critical vulnerability in Mixture-of-Experts architectures where sparse routing can bypass safety training by activating different expert combinations during adversarial attacks versus normal operation. Their solution leverages shared experts that activate consistently across inputs to maintain safety alignment. This addresses a growing concern as MoE becomes the dominant scaling architecture for frontier models.

**[Before the Script, Set the Stage: How Worldview Simulation Amplifies Psychologically Grounded Persuasion in Multi-Turn Jailbreaking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02414v1)** introduces BLUEPRINT, a framework that separates social influence tactics from situational context modeling to optimize multi-turn jailbreak attacks. The work demonstrates that creating immersive worldview simulations significantly amplifies the effectiveness of psychological manipulation techniques across multiple frontier models. This reveals sophisticated attack vectors that traditional single-turn safety measures fail to address.

**[Improving Evaluation Realism with Inference-Time Compute and Deployment Scaffolds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02302v1)** tackles the fundamental problem of "evaluation awareness" - where capable models can distinguish between testing and deployment contexts, undermining safety evaluations. Their techniques make simulated evaluations harder to distinguish from real deployments through inference-time compute allocation and deployment scaffolding. This work is critical for developing reliable safety evaluations of increasingly capable systems.

**[FUSE: An Evaluating Framework for Dangerous Capabilities of LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02168v1)** presents a unified evaluation framework that assesses dangerous capabilities through Knowledge, Defense, and Harm pipelines, producing standardized capability profiles. The modular design allows consistent evaluation across domains while the core engine remains unchanged, addressing the current fragmentation in safety evaluation approaches. This framework could become essential for systematic capability assessment as models become more powerful.

**[The Shape of Ownership: Verifying LLM Provenance through Semantic Structures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02553v1)** addresses model ownership verification in an era of opaque APIs and model redistribution. Unlike existing fingerprinting methods that rely on memorized associations, this approach uses semantic structural analysis that remains robust to fine-tuning and other modifications. This work becomes increasingly important as LLMs are deployed behind APIs where traditional ownership verification methods fail.

## Emerging Themes and Implications

**Multi-Turn and Contextual Safety Risks**: Several papers highlight how safety mechanisms designed for single interactions fail in multi-turn contexts. The research on worldview simulation and multi-turn jailbreaking reveals sophisticated attack vectors that exploit conversational dynamics, suggesting current safety training may be fundamentally insufficient for agentic deployments.

**Architectural Vulnerabilities**: The work on Mixture-of-Experts safety and skill-based policy steering reveals how modern architectural choices create new attack surfaces. As models become more modular and efficient, these architectural decisions introduce security considerations that weren't present in monolithic designs.

**Evaluation Reliability Crisis**: Multiple papers address fundamental challenges in AI safety evaluation - from evaluation awareness undermining test validity to linguistic illegibility hiding true model reasoning. This suggests a growing recognition that our current evaluation methods may be inadequate for assessing the safety of increasingly capable systems.

**Supply Chain and Provenance Concerns**: The research on skill-based attacks and model ownership verification highlights emerging risks as AI systems become more modular and distributed. These supply chain vulnerabilities could become critical as organizations increasingly rely on third-party components and model-as-a-service deployments.

The convergence of these themes suggests we're entering a new phase of AI safety research where traditional approaches may be insufficient for the complexity and capability of emerging systems. The research points toward a need for more sophisticated, multi-faceted approaches to safety that account for architectural choices, deployment contexts, and the increasingly sophisticated nature of potential adversaries.
